Takeaway: AI is already replacing the routine tasks that used to fill an executive assistant's day, and the number of purely administrative roles is shrinking. It's not replacing the judgment, trust, and relationship work at the center of a strong EA's value. The assistants who thrive are the ones using AI to do more of the work that only a person can do.

If you support an executive for a living, the headlines are hard to ignore. Reports name administrative and secretarial roles among the fastest declining jobs of the decade. Tools now schedule meetings, draft emails, summarize calls, and organize inboxes. It's reasonable to wonder whether the role has a future.

The clear-eyed answer is that the routine parts of the job are genuinely at risk, and the human parts aren't. Understanding exactly where that line falls is what lets you plan a career instead of worrying about one.

What the data actually says

Two things are true at the same time, and both matter.

The number of administrative roles is declining. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 lists clerical and secretarial workers, including administrative assistants and executive secretaries, among the roles expected to see the largest decline in absolute numbers between 2025 and 2030. The largest projected decline for any single role is cashiers and ticket clerks. Administrative assistants and executive secretaries come second, a grouping the report expects to shed roughly 6.1 million jobs on net by 2030, driven by AI, broadening digital access, and automation. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects little or no change in employment for secretaries and administrative assistants over the 2024 to 2034 decade, essentially flat (about 12,400 fewer jobs, or roughly zero percent) rather than sharp growth or collapse (BLS).

The longer trend is stark. As the Associated Press reported, in a story Fortune later syndicated, the number of administrative assistants in the United States fell from about 3.5 million in 2004 to roughly 2.1 million in 2024, a decline of around 40 percent even as the overall workforce grew (Fortune).

There's a reasonable case that the pressure falls hardest on routine work rather than senior support, though the official numbers don't draw that line cleanly. The tasks most exposed are the repetitive ones: data entry, basic scheduling, standard correspondence, filing. Roles that center on judgment, discretion, and relationships, which is what an executive assistant to a senior leader provides, may be less exposed, because the value there isn't task execution but trust and anticipation, and those don't automate cleanly. Importantly, the data doesn't yet confirm a senior-level safe harbor: BLS projects the executive secretary and executive administrative assistant category to decline by roughly 1.6 percent over the decade, about the same as the broader non-medical secretary category. What AI genuinely does well

It helps to be specific rather than vague. AI is strong at exactly the parts of the job that consumed hours and produced little visible strategic value:

  • Scheduling and coordination. Finding times, proposing slots, and handling routine back-and-forth.
  • Drafting. First drafts of emails, agendas, briefs, and summaries that a person then edits and approves.
  • Meeting capture. Transcribing calls, producing notes, and surfacing action items. One administrative professional quoted in that same Associated Press report described cutting meeting-notes work from hours to under five minutes, which freed her to participate in the meeting instead of just documenting it (Fortune).
  • Information triage. Sorting, tagging, and summarizing inbound information so a person can decide faster.
  • Research and analysis. Pulling together background, comparing options, and structuring data for a decision.

Notice what these have in common. They're all inputs to a decision, not the decision. They're drafts, not final calls. That distinction is the whole story.

What AI cannot do

The core of a senior EA's value sits in places current AI doesn't reach.

Trust and discretion. An executive shares unfinished thinking, sensitive personnel matters, and half-formed decisions with an assistant. That relationship runs on judgment about what to share, when, and with whom. It can't be delegated to a tool that has no accountability and no relationship to protect.

Reading the room and the person. A strong EA knows their executive is running hot before a hard meeting and quietly reshapes the day. They catch the tension in a hallway exchange and follow up before it becomes a problem. That anticipation comes from paying attention to specific humans over time. As the founder of one AI-forward company put it, even after mandating AI use across her team, the technology couldn't replace her executive assistant, because the role had evolved toward relationship-building, strategic thinking, and understanding organizational dynamics (Fortune).

Judgment under ambiguity. Deciding which of two important people gets the executive's limited time this week, when a request is really a symptom of a different problem, or when to protect the calendar and when to break it open, are all judgment calls that depend on context a model doesn't hold.

Owning a relationship on the executive's behalf. When an EA manages a board member, a key client, or a difficult stakeholder as an extension of their principal, they're trading on a human relationship. That's force-multiplying work, the kind we describe in what a force multiplier really is, and it's the opposite of a commodity task.

How the strongest EAs are using AI

The assistants who appear to be adapting best aren't resisting these tools. They're using them to reclaim hours from low-value work and reinvest that time in higher-value work.

Industry surveys already show meaningful adoption. The American Society of Administrative Professionals' State of the Profession research found that a large majority of administrative professionals now use AI on the job, up sharply year over year, with executive-support roles among the heavier adopters. The pattern in practice looks like this:

  1. Automate the repetitive layer. Let AI handle first-draft scheduling, note-taking, summaries, and routine drafting.
  2. Redeploy the time. Use the reclaimed hours for relationship management, project ownership, anticipating needs, and taking real work off the executive's plate.
  3. Move up the value chain. Take on the coordination and light project management that starts to resemble a chief of staff's remit. That progression is the heart of the EA-to-chief-of-staff path.

The through-line is simple. AI can raise the floor on what is expected, because the routine tasks are getting cheaper, and it can raise the ceiling on what an assistant accomplishes once they're no longer buried in those tasks. The more hopeful reading is that the role shifts upward rather than disappears, but that outcome isn't automatic. It depends on assistants and their employers actually making the move.

What this means for your career

If you support an executive, treat AI as a skill to build, not a threat to avoid. A few concrete moves:

  • Get genuinely fluent with the tools. Learn to prompt well, to check AI output for errors, and to build small workflows that save real time. This is now a baseline expectation, not a bonus.
  • Invest in the human skills. Judgment, communication, stakeholder management, and discretion are the parts of the job that hold their value. They're also the hardest to build, which is why they compound.
  • Move toward ownership. Volunteer for the project, the process, the relationship. Every step from task-doer toward owner moves you further from what AI replaces and closer to what leaders will always pay for.
  • Make your value visible. When you save time with AI, use it to take on work that clearly matters, and make sure your executive sees the shift.

The assistants most exposed over the next decade are likely the ones whose value was defined entirely by tasks a tool can now do. The ones best positioned are those who were always doing more than tasks, and who now have better tools to do it with. Neither outcome is guaranteed, but it's the direction the current evidence points.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will AI replace executive assistants entirely?

No, not the senior version of the role. AI is replacing routine administrative tasks and shrinking the number of purely clerical jobs, and reports like the World Economic Forum's project large declines in administrative and secretarial employment through 2030 (WEF). But the judgment, trust, and relationship work at the heart of supporting a senior executive isn't something current AI can do, which is why even AI-forward leaders report they still rely on their assistants (Fortune).

Which parts of the EA job is AI most likely to take over?

The repetitive input tasks: routine scheduling, first-draft emails and agendas, meeting transcription and notes, information sorting, and background research. These are exactly the tasks that used to consume hours without producing visible strategic value.

Is the executive assistant job actually declining?

The purely administrative version is. The number of administrative assistants in the U.S. fell roughly 40 percent from 2004 to 2024 (Fortune), and the BLS projects little or no change for the category over the 2024 to 2034 decade (BLS). Senior EA roles built on judgment and relationships may prove more durable, though BLS still projects the executive category itself to decline modestly (about 1.6 percent), so this is a reasonable expectation rather than a guarantee.

How should an executive assistant use AI without being replaced by it?

Use AI to automate the routine layer, then redeploy the reclaimed time into higher-value work: relationship management, anticipating needs, and owning projects. Fluency with the tools is now a baseline skill, and pairing it with strong human judgment is what makes an EA hard to replace.

Could AI actually make the EA role better?

For many assistants it already has. Offloading note-taking and routine drafting frees time to participate in meetings, take real work off an executive's plate, and move toward chief-of-staff-style ownership. AI raises the expected floor on routine tasks and raises the ceiling on what a great assistant can accomplish.
