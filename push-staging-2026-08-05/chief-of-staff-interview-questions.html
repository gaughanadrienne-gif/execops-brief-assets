Takeaway: A chief of staff interview is less a test of what you know and more a test of your judgment, your communication, and whether one specific leader can trust you close to them. Prepare by mapping your best ownership stories to the STAR structure, researching the principal as carefully as you would research the company, and being ready to show how you think under pressure.

Chief of staff interviews trip people up because they don't follow the usual script. There's rarely a technical screen with a clean right answer. Instead, you get a series of open-ended, situational, and behavioral questions designed to see how you reason, how you handle ambiguity, and how you would operate as an extension of the person across the table. The interviewer isn't filling a slot on an org chart. They're deciding whether to hand you their calendar, their confidences, and their reputation.

This guide covers the questions that come up most, what each one is actually testing, how to structure your answers so they land, and the preparation that separates strong candidates from the rest.

What a chief of staff interview is really testing

Before the questions, understand what the conversation is for. A chief of staff has little formal authority and operates almost entirely through trust and influence. Dan Ciampa's Harvard Business Review framing describes the role as part air traffic controller, part integrator across siloed work streams, part honest broker, and part confidant (Harvard Business Review). Every question in the interview is a probe into whether you can play those parts.

In practice, interviewers are testing five things: your judgment under ambiguity, your ability to communicate clearly and concisely, your skill at getting things done across teams you don't control, your discretion with sensitive information, and your fit with this particular leader's working style. Keep those five in mind and most questions become easy to decode.

The most common question categories

Published question banks for the role, including the Chief of Staff Network's collection of interview questions, cluster into a handful of recurring themes (The Chief of Staff Network). Here are the categories, what each tests, and examples you should expect.

Prioritization and judgment

  • "The CEO has five urgent priorities and time for two. How do you decide?"
  • "Walk me through how you would spend your first 90 days in this role."

What it tests: whether you can sequence competing demands, make a defensible call quickly, and think about the leader's time as the scarce resource it is. Strong answers show a repeatable way of deciding, not a gut reaction. Ground your first-90-days answer in a real plan, which we cover in our first 90 days operating plan guide.

Influence without authority

  • "Tell me about a time you drove a cross-functional project without owning the teams involved."
  • "How would you get a department head to change course when they don't report to you and disagree with you?"

What it tests: the core mechanic of the job. You will spend most of your time moving work through people who don't answer to you. Interviewers want evidence you can build alignment and follow-through without pulling rank. Our guide to influence without authority breaks down the moves.

Communication and executive presence

  • "How would you deliver bad news to the CEO?"
  • "Summarize a complex situation you managed in under a minute."

What it tests: whether you can distill signal from noise and communicate up with candor and composure. Some interviewers will deliberately ask you to be concise to see if you can.

Handling conflict and difficult people

  • "Describe a time you disagreed with your executive. What did you do?"
  • "How do you handle a situation where two senior leaders are in open conflict?"

What it tests: your role as honest broker. A good chief of staff can push back privately, represent the leader fairly, and defuse tension between executives without taking a side that damages trust.

Discretion and ethics

  • "You learn something confidential that affects a colleague. How do you handle it?"
  • "The CEO asks you to do something you think is a mistake. What do you do?"

What it tests: whether you can be trusted with the sensitive context the role carries every day. The role runs on discretion, and interviewers watch closely for how you reason about competing loyalties.

Motivation and fit

  • "Why do you want to be a chief of staff, and why for this leader?"
  • "This role is often a two to three year seat, not a permanent job. How does that fit your goals?"

What it tests: whether you understand the role for what it is and want it for the right reasons. A weak answer treats the title as a promotion. A strong answer shows you understand the work is broad, ambiguous, and usually time-bound.

How to structure your answers with STAR

For any behavioral or situational question, the STAR method keeps your answer clear and prevents you from rambling. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, and Result (MIT Career Advising & Professional Development).

  • Situation. Set the scene briefly. Keep this to roughly a fifth of your answer.
  • Task. Explain what you were responsible for and what was at stake.
  • Action. Describe the specific steps you took. This should be the largest part of your answer, and the focus should stay on what you personally did, not what the team did (MIT).
  • Result. Share the outcome, ideally with a concrete number or a clear before-and-after, and what you learned.

Aim for roughly one to two minutes per answer. Practice out loud so the stories sound natural rather than memorized.

A worked STAR example

Question: "Tell me about a time you drove a cross-functional project without formal authority."

Situation. "Two departments, sales and product, were blaming each other for missed launch dates, and the CEO was tired of hearing about it in every leadership meeting."

Task. "I was asked to get the next launch out on time, but I had no authority over either team."

Action. "I mapped the actual handoff points between the two teams, found the three where work was stalling, and set up a single weekly 20-minute sync with clear owners and dates for each handoff. I wrote a one-page status doc that both leads reviewed before the meeting so we spent the time on decisions, not updates. When one owner slipped twice, I raised it directly and privately rather than in the group, and reset expectations."

Result. "The launch shipped two days early, the first on-time launch in four quarters. The weekly sync became the standing operating rhythm for launches after that."

That answer demonstrates prioritization, influence without authority, communication, and follow-through in ninety seconds. Prepare four or five stories like this that you can flex to different questions.

Preparing the stories before you walk in

Don't improvise your examples in the room. Before the interview, write down four to six situations from your career that show ownership end to end, then tag each with the skills it demonstrates: prioritization, influence, communication, conflict, discretion, execution. Most good stories cover more than one, which lets you reuse them across questions.

Pick examples where you carried something to a finished result, not where you produced a recommendation and handed it off. A chief of staff owns outcomes, and your stories should prove it. Lead with the unglamorous follow-through, because that's exactly the reassurance a principal is looking for.

Research the principal, not just the company

This is the step most candidates skip, and it's the one that matters most for this role. You're interviewing to work as an extension of one person. Understanding that person is the assignment.

In the classic HBR article Managing Your Boss, Gabarro and Kotter argue that being genuinely useful to a leader starts with a clear read on their strengths, weaknesses, working style, and pressures (Harvard Business Review). Bring that lens into your preparation. Read the principal's interviews, posts, letters to shareholders, or podcast appearances. Understand what the company is under pressure to do this year and where this leader is most stretched. Then shape your answers to show how you would relieve that specific pressure.

It also gives you sharper questions to ask them, which is part of the evaluation. Strong closing questions for a chief of staff interview include: "What does success look like in this role in the first six months?" "What would you want off your plate that you can't delegate today?" and "How do you prefer to receive bad news?" Those questions signal that you already think like the role.

Common mistakes that sink candidates

  • Answering strategy questions with opinions instead of a method. Interviewers want to see how you decide, not just what you would decide. Show your reasoning.
  • Talking about the team when the question is about you. The STAR action step should stay focused on your specific contribution (MIT).
  • Treating the role as a stepping stone out loud. It usually is one, but leading with that reads as using the leader rather than serving them.
  • Skipping the principal research. Generic answers about the company signal that you haven't grasped what the job actually is.
  • Rambling. Concise is a competency here. If you can't summarize a situation in a minute, you have shown the interviewer something.

Frequently asked questions

What questions are asked in a chief of staff interview?

Expect open-ended situational and behavioral questions across a few themes: prioritization and judgment, influence without authority, executive communication, handling conflict and difficult people, discretion and ethics, and motivation and fit (The Chief of Staff Network). Common examples include how you would spend your first 90 days, how you would deliver bad news to the CEO, and a time you drove a cross-functional project without authority.

How do I prepare for a chief of staff interview?

Prepare four to six ownership stories that show you carried work to a finished result, tag each with the skills it demonstrates, and practice delivering them in the STAR structure in under two minutes (MIT). Then research the principal as carefully as the company, since you're interviewing to work as an extension of one person (Harvard Business Review).

What does a chief of staff interview test?

It tests judgment under ambiguity, clear and concise communication, the ability to get work done across teams you don't control, discretion with sensitive information, and fit with the specific leader's working style. Because the role carries little formal authority and lives on trust, the interview is designed to see whether the principal can rely on you close to them (Harvard Business Review).

How do I answer "how would you spend your first 90 days" as a chief of staff?

Show a listening-first plan rather than sweeping changes. Describe understanding the principal and the leadership team, mapping how the organization actually runs, and identifying the two or three priorities that lack a clear owner before you move anything. Ground it in a real structure, like the one in our first 90 days operating plan.

How important is researching the executive before a chief of staff interview?

It's one of the highest-leverage things you can do. You're interviewing to be an extension of one person, and being useful to a leader starts with understanding their strengths, weaknesses, working style, and pressures (Harvard Business Review). Reading their public interviews, letters, and posts lets you shape answers around the specific problems they need solved.
