Takeaway: Executive assistant careers grow in two directions: deeper, toward supporting more senior leaders as a senior or lead EA, and wider, toward operations and chief of staff roles that own outcomes rather than tasks. In both directions, the move is the same: shift from doing tasks to owning problems, and make that shift visible.

The executive assistant role is often described as a ceiling. It's closer to a launchpad. The skills that make a strong EA, judgment, discretion, coordination, and the ability to make a busy leader more effective, are exactly the skills that senior operations and chief of staff roles run on. The question isn't whether an EA career can grow. It's which direction you want it to grow, and what to build to get there.

This is a map of the ladder and the moves that climb it.

Two directions the career can grow

Most EA growth follows one of two paths, and plenty of people combine them.

Deeper: the senior support track. You keep the core of the EA role but take it up-market, supporting more senior and higher-stakes leaders, eventually a CEO or a founder, and often taking a senior or lead title. This track rewards depth of judgment, trust, and mastery of complex support. A seasoned executive assistant to a CEO operates as a genuine extension of their principal, and the pay reflects it, as our EA salary guide lays out.

Wider: the operations track. You expand beyond support into owning projects, processes, and outcomes, moving toward roles like operations manager, business manager, or chief of staff. This track trades some of the pure support work for broader authority over how the organization runs. The clearest version is the EA-to-chief-of-staff path, a well-worn route precisely because the two roles share so much DNA.

Neither is better. They suit different people. The deep track fits those who love being the indispensable right hand to a leader. The wide track fits those who want to own outcomes and eventually lead operations themselves.

The rungs of the ladder

Titles vary by company, but the progression tends to look like this.

  • Administrative assistant. Entry-level support: scheduling, correspondence, logistics, often supporting a team or a mid-level manager.
  • Executive assistant. Dedicated support to a senior leader, with more autonomy, discretion, and complexity.
  • Senior or lead executive assistant. Supporting top executives, often mentoring other assistants or coordinating an EA team, and owning meaningful projects alongside support.
  • Chief of staff, business manager, or operations lead. Owning outcomes and priorities rather than tasks, working across the organization, and running on borrowed authority and earned trust.

Two things about this ladder are worth naming. First, the routine administrative rung is under pressure. Both the BLS and the World Economic Forum project declining demand for purely clerical and administrative roles through the 2030s, driven largely by automation (BLS, WEF). That makes climbing toward judgment-based, harder-to-automate work a matter of career resilience, not just ambition. Second, the jump from senior EA to chief of staff is a real step up in seniority, not a lateral retitle. McKinsey found people entering chief of staff roles bring, on average, just over 12 years of experience (McKinsey). It's reachable from an EA seat, but it's earned, not automatic.

The core move: from tasks to ownership

Every meaningful step up the ladder is a version of the same shift. You stop being measured by tasks completed and start being measured by problems owned. Practically, that looks like:

  • Volunteering for the project, not just the logistics. When your executive needs an offsite planned, own the outcome and the follow-through, not only the room booking.
  • Building systems, not just handling requests. Turn a recurring problem into a process. An assistant who installs an operating cadence or a single source of truth is already doing operations work.
  • Anticipating instead of reacting. Move from answering needs to seeing them coming. Anticipation is the skill that separates a good assistant from an indispensable one, and it's the foundation of force-multiplying work.
  • Taking real work off the executive's plate. Not tasks they hand you, but decisions and follow-through they would otherwise carry themselves.

Every one of these moves you toward the durable, higher-value end of the career and away from the routine work that automation is steadily absorbing. It's also the exact behavior that gets you noticed for the next role.

Skills worth building

Growth is easier when you deliberately build the skills the next rung requires.

  • Project and process management. The ability to run a cross-functional project to completion is the single most transferable operations skill. Learn to scope, sequence, and drive work across teams.
  • Communication and influence. Senior operations roles run on influence, not authority. Writing clear briefs, running effective meetings, and managing stakeholders are core, not optional.
  • Business and financial literacy. Understanding how your organization makes money, reads its numbers, and sets priorities lets you contribute to decisions rather than just support them.
  • AI and tools fluency. As routine tasks automate, fluency with modern tools is now a baseline expectation. Use it to reclaim time for higher-value work rather than treating it as a threat.
  • Judgment and discretion. The hardest to teach and the most valuable. It compounds with every high-stakes situation you handle well.

Making your growth visible

Building skills is half the job. Being seen doing it is the other half. Careers stall not only when people stop growing but when their growth stays invisible.

  • Tell your executive what you want. A leader who knows you're aiming for a chief of staff or senior operations role can hand you the assignments that build toward it. One who doesn't will keep handing you what you already do well.
  • Keep a record of owned outcomes. Track the projects you drove and what they produced. This is the raw material for a raise, a promotion, or a new role.
  • Reposition your professional presence. Your resume and LinkedIn should read like an operator's, foregrounding outcomes and ownership, not a list of administrative duties. Our guide on resume and LinkedIn for exec-ops covers exactly how.

A realistic timeline

There's no fixed clock, but a common arc looks like this: a few years establishing yourself as a reliable EA, then a deliberate stretch, one to three years, taking on projects and ownership beyond your job description, then a move up, either to a senior support role or into operations. The people who move fastest aren't the ones who wait to be promoted. They're the ones who start doing the next role's work before they hold the title, then make the case that the title should catch up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an executive assistant become a chief of staff?

Yes, and it's one of the most natural transitions in operations, because the roles share so much: proximity to a leader, coordination, discretion, and the job of making an executive more effective. It's a genuine step up in seniority rather than a lateral move; McKinsey found chiefs of staff average just over 12 years of experience entering the role (McKinsey). Our EA-to-chief-of-staff guide covers the specifics.

What is the next step up from executive assistant?

There are two directions. Deeper, toward senior or lead EA and supporting more senior leaders. Or wider, toward operations roles like business manager, operations lead, or chief of staff, which own outcomes rather than tasks. Which is right depends on whether you want to be the indispensable right hand to a leader or to own how the organization runs.

Is the executive assistant role a dead end?

No, but the purely administrative version of it is under pressure from automation, with the BLS and WEF projecting declining demand for routine clerical work (BLS, WEF). The judgment-based, ownership-heavy end of the career is durable and growing in value. The path forward is to climb toward work that only a person can do.

What skills should I build to advance as an EA?

Project and process management, communication and influence, business and financial literacy, fluency with modern tools including AI, and above all judgment and discretion. Project management is the most transferable skill into operations roles, and judgment is the most valuable because it's the hardest to automate or teach.

Do I need a certification to grow as an executive assistant?

Not for the senior support track, where experience and track record matter most. For the move into a chief of staff role, a targeted program can help accelerate the transition and signal intent, though it's one option among several. We compare the main programs in our certifications guide.
